# NANO TECH — EPISODE 1: "FIRST BOOT"

## September 1, 2026 — Dallas/Fort Worth International Airport, Texas

---

The jetway accordion-folded against the fuselage of the Philippine Airlines 777, and thirteen hours of recycled cabin air released its grip on Joziah Martin's lungs in one long, controlled exhale. He stepped into the terminal at 2:47 PM Central Daylight Time, and Texas hit him before he cleared the gate — not the heat, not yet, but the scale. The ceilings were enormous. The terminal was wider than the entire domestic building at Ninoy Aquino. Everything in this country was built like it expected more people than it currently held.

He carried one oversized checked bag, a hard-shell carry-on, and a single backpack. That was everything he owned that mattered. The rest — the improvised tools, the secondhand parts bins, the folding workbench he'd welded himself from scrap angle iron — he'd left behind. Not with anyone. Just behind.

**Joziah "Ace" Martin. Twenty-eight years old. Six-three, a hundred kilos, lean and cut in a way that made the Customs officer glance twice and then glance a third time when he spoke.**

"Purpose of your visit?"

"Relocation." His voice came out the way it always did — low, unhurried, smooth enough that the woman behind the glass paused with her stamp half-raised. Not performed. Not put on. Just the voice he'd been given. "I'm opening an automotive repair and restoration business."

She stamped his passport. "Welcome to Texas."

He collected his bag from the carousel — a duffel so heavy with tools that he swung it onto his shoulder one-handed, and a businessman waiting for his Tumi watched the motion and quietly recalculated his assumptions about the lean Filipino guy in the plain black t-shirt — and walked out through the automatic doors into the arrivals pickup lane.

The heat hit him like opening an oven.

September in DFW. Ninety-six degrees. Humidity thick enough to chew. He'd researched it, he'd expected it, and it still landed like a wall. The nano machine adjusted before he'd finished his first full breath outside — core temperature stabilizing, sweat response optimized, the faint burn of UV on his forearms dampened to nothing. By the time he reached the rideshare pickup zone, his body had already decided that 96°F was acceptable operating temperature.

He didn't need it to. He'd worked in Philippine heat his whole life. But it was there, and it was always on, and it had been part of him long enough that the line between what he could handle and what the billions of microscopic units threaded through every muscle fiber and nerve cluster handled for him was a line he'd stopped trying to draw.

**$20,000.** That was the number. Savings from years of underpaid electronics repair, computer builds, and scrappy vehicle work in a country where none of those things paid well. Converted, transferred, and sitting in a U.S. checking account he'd opened remotely through an online bank that accepted his visa status. Twenty thousand dollars to start a business, find a place to live, buy tools, buy a first project, build a YouTube channel, and survive in a state he'd never set foot in until sixty seconds ago.

No family to wire money home to. No family to wire money from. No friend picking him up at arrivals. No one who knew he'd landed except the U.S. government and the airline.

He pulled out his phone and opened the Uber app.

---

## The Ride — DFW to Fort Worth

He'd chosen the Dallas–Fort Worth metroplex deliberately, and within it, he was leaning toward the **Fort Worth side**. Not Dallas proper. The reasoning was precise:

**Fort Worth** had the truck-and-muscle-car culture baked deeper into its identity. Dallas leaned business-casual, German luxury, and Uptown brunch. Fort Worth leaned cattle, Stockyards, rodeo, and the kind of people who kept a project truck in the side yard and knew what a 7.3 Powerstroke sounded like at idle. The car scene split accordingly: Dallas had more exotics and euro meets. Fort Worth and the surrounding sprawl — Weatherford, Azle, Burleson, Mansfield, the stretch down toward Cleburne — had more project cars, more shade-tree mechanics, more guys who needed a good independent shop and didn't want to pay dealer labor rates.

That was his market. Not the six-figure restoration clients. Not yet. The guy with a 2004 F-150 that won't start. The woman with a 2012 Camaro SS she bought off Marketplace that's throwing a check-engine light nobody can diagnose. The flipper who picked up a flood-titled Charger from Copart and needs someone to tell him whether it's saveable.

Beyond the client base: Fort Worth had legal card clubs within driving distance (the Poker House, Texas Card House had locations, and several others operated in the DFW area), multiple airsoft fields (D14 Airsoft in Saginaw was close, and others dotted the metroplex), and the drag-racing scene stretched from Texas Motorplex in Ennis to smaller eighth-mile tracks and street-legal events across North Texas.

Land was also cheaper on the Fort Worth side. Not cheap — nothing in DFW was truly cheap anymore — but the further west or south you went from downtown, the more two-acre lots started appearing in the listings at prices that weren't immediately laughable.

His Uber driver was a heavyset Black man in his fifties named Gerald who drove a well-maintained 2019 Chevy Suburban and who, upon hearing Joziah's destination — a budget extended-stay motel in the Camp Bowie / West 7th corridor area of Fort Worth — and his reason for being in Texas, spent the entire 45-minute drive offering unsolicited but genuinely useful advice.

"You want to flip cars, you need a dealer license before you do more than a handful. Texas don't play about that. TxDMV. You need a bond, you need a location, you need insurance. It's not hard, it's just paperwork, but if you skip it they'll come after you."

"I know," Ace said. "I've read the statute. It's roughly five in a year before they consider you a dealer, but the actual line is whether you're buying and selling as a business. I plan to get the GDN before I hit that."

Gerald glanced in the rearview. Most passengers didn't talk like that. Most passengers who looked like that — young, built, with a face and a voice that belonged on a screen — definitely didn't talk like that.

"You done your homework."

"I've had time." Ace watched the Texas landscape unroll outside the window. Flat. Wide. Endless sky. Strip malls and barbecue joints and transmission shops and self-storage facilities and churches, all of it sprawled out along six-lane roads with speed limits that would've been highway speeds in Manila. "What's the best parts-sourcing around here? I mean for used. Pull-a-part yards, that kind of thing."

Gerald lit up. For the remaining thirty minutes he delivered a masterclass in North Texas junkyard geography — LKQ Pick Your Part locations, U-Pull-&-Pay, the independent yards along the I-35W corridor south toward Burleson, the Copart lots in Dallas and Fort Worth — and Ace logged every name and address with perfect recall, the nano machine filing each one into a spatial map of the metroplex that built itself in real time behind his eyes, cross-referenced with satellite imagery he'd studied on the flight.

When they pulled up to the **InTown Suites** on Camp Bowie West — $249/week, furnished, kitchenette, the kind of place that smelled like industrial cleaner and other people's decisions — Gerald turned around fully.

"Hey. What'd you say the name of your shop was gonna be?"

"Nano Tech."

"Nano Tech." Gerald chewed on it. "That's different. Sounds like computers."

"I used to fix those too."

"Well, listen. My daughter's got an '09 Accord that's been pulling to the right and eating front tires for six months. Every alignment shop tells her it's fine. If you get set up and you're not too expensive, I'll send her your way."

Ace pulled his single bag out of the Suburban's cargo area — one-handed again, the weight nothing — and leaned back to the driver's window. "I'm not set up yet. But when I am, first alignment's on me."

Gerald laughed. "Boy, you don't give away free work on your first week."

"I won't be giving it away. I'll be proving something."

He watched the Suburban pull away, and then he stood alone in the parking lot of a $249-a-week motel in Fort Worth, Texas, at 3:52 PM on September 1, 2026, with a bag on his shoulder and $20,000 in the bank and not a single person on the planet who would notice if he didn't wake up tomorrow.

## InTown Suites — Room 14 — Evening

The room was twelve feet by fourteen feet. A full bed with a comforter that had been washed into submission. A kitchenette with a two-burner stove, a mini fridge, and a microwave. A bathroom with a shower stall that would require him to turn sideways. A window unit air conditioner that hummed at a frequency his nano-enhanced hearing immediately catalogued as a worn compressor bearing — not failing yet, but within a year.

He set his bag down, sat on the edge of the bed, and began.

**The nano machine didn't need to be activated. It was always active.** But there was a difference between its passive state — monitoring vitals, filtering toxins, maintaining his body at peak function — and the aggressive engagement he was about to run.

He closed his eyes and opened every channel.

---

### Phase 1: The City Scan

Not literally scanning Fort Worth. But pulling together everything he'd researched over the past six months into a single operational picture, augmented by the nano machine's information-transfer function converting raw data into spatial, financial, and strategic knowledge he could act on immediately.

**Housing:** the InTown Suites would burn $1,080/month. That was acceptable for thirty to sixty days while he found something better. A room in a shared house or a cheap apartment could drop that to $600–$800. Long term, housing was a runway problem, not a comfort problem. He didn't get tired. He didn't need recovery sleep the way a normal body did. The room was a mailing address and a place to shower.

**Shop space:** this was the critical path. He needed a bay. Options, ranked:

1. **Rent a single bay in an existing shop.** Some independent shops in the Fort Worth area rented unused bays to solo mechanics. $800–$1,500/month depending on location and what came with it (lift, air, electricity). Cheapest path to working immediately, but limited space and someone else's rules.
2. **Rent a small standalone garage or warehouse unit.** $1,200–$2,500/month for a two-bay or small warehouse space in the industrial areas south or west of Fort Worth (Benbrook, White Settlement, the Haltom City corridor). More freedom, more overhead. Might come with nothing — he'd need to source his own lift, compressor, and lighting.
3. **Mobile / driveway work.** Legal in Texas with restrictions. Some cities had ordinances against visible vehicle repair in residential areas. No overhead, but no credibility, no lift, and brutal in the September heat. Not a real option for the business he wanted to build.

He needed to see spaces in person. Tomorrow.

**Tools:** he'd left his Philippine tools behind because most of them were improvised junk that wouldn't meet U.S. standards. He needed a base toolkit: metric and SAE socket sets, wrenches, screwdrivers, pliers, a torque wrench, a breaker bar, a floor jack, jack stands, a basic scan tool (though the nano machine was a better scan tool than anything on the market), an impact wrench, and eventually a welder. Harbor Freight for the budget tier. A decent starter set could run $1,500–$2,500. A used professional set from a retiring mechanic or a pawn shop could save money if he found one.

**First project vehicle:** he needed something to work on, film, and either keep as a shop truck or flip for seed money. Budget: $3,000–$5,000, leaving $15,000–$17,000 for rent, tools, and survival. Facebook Marketplace, Craigslist, and Copart were the hunting grounds. He wanted something with a strong story — a neglected truck, a barn find, a salvage-title muscle car — that would make good first-episode content.

**YouTube:** he needed a camera (his phone was adequate for early content, but a used Sony or Canon mirrorless would elevate it fast — $400–$800 used), a tripod, a lavalier mic, and basic editing software. He already knew how to edit from his tech background. DaVinci Resolve was free. The channel name was decided: **Nano Tech**. He'd register it tonight.

**Texas business formation:** he needed to register Nano Tech as a business entity (likely a sole proprietorship initially, then an LLC), get a sales-tax permit from the Texas Comptroller, and eventually — once flipping volume justified it — apply for the GDN through TxDMV. That required a surety bond ($25,000 bond, which cost a fraction of face value as a premium — roughly $100–$400/year for a new applicant), a qualifying business location, liability insurance, and a completed application. He wasn't there yet. But the clock started with his first flip.

**Visa:** his immigration status was settled — he had legal authorization to live and work in the United States. The specifics were part of his pre-move preparation and were in order. This was not a thread that would create legal jeopardy.

He opened his eyes. The room was dark. He hadn't turned on the lights. The nano machine's enhanced vision rendered everything in clean detail anyway — the texture of the ceiling tile, the hairline crack in the window frame, the dust motes suspended in the last band of daylight cutting through the blinds.

He pulled out his phone.

The math was simple and unforgiving. He had to generate revenue within sixty days or the dream died in a budget motel room in Fort Worth with no one to call.

---

He sat on the bed in the dark. The air conditioner hummed its worn-bearing hum. Through the thin wall he could hear a TV playing a rerun of something with a laugh track. Through the window, the amber glow of a Fort Worth strip mall at night: a Whataburger, a vape shop, a title-loan office, and a self-storage place. The American dream in miniature, lit by sodium vapor.

He was alone. Completely, structurally alone. Not in the way that people said they were alone when they meant they were lonely — he had no framework of people to be lonely *from*. No mother to disappoint, no father to prove something to, no friend to text "I made it." The silence in his phone was not an absence. It was the shape of his life.

The nano machine hummed in the background of his nervous system, billions of units maintaining, optimizing, and preparing. It could scan an engine block through solid metal, track cards through a shuffled deck, and predict where an airsoft BB would land before the trigger was pulled. It could heal a third-degree burn in minutes and let him lift a transmission with his bare hands.

It could not manufacture a single person who gave a damn whether Joziah Martin succeeded or failed.

That part was on him.

He lay back on the bed, stared at the ceiling, and began planning tomorrow. The nano machine didn't need him to sleep, but he closed his eyes anyway, because some human rituals were worth keeping even when they weren't required. The darkness behind his eyelids was the same darkness he'd stared at on the last night in the Philippines, in a room that was smaller than this one and emptier in ways that had nothing to do with furniture.

Tomorrow he would see three shops and three vehicles. He would shake hands with strangers who had no reason to trust him. He would start building something from nothing in a state that didn't know his name.

**September 1, 2026. Day one. Nano Tech. Fort Worth, Texas.**
